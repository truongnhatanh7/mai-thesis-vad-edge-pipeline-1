Literature Review

How do lightweight vision preprocessing and OS-level optimization, individually and jointly, affect the robustness and real-time performance of CPU-only public-safety inference on edge devices?

Introduction
Public safety increasingly relies on surveillance systems to detect suspicious or abnormal activities. To reduce continuous human monitoring, many systems use AI models deployed on edge devices. However, their performance can degrade under challenging conditions such as poor lighting and low-quality video, while resource-constrained hardware can also limit latency, throughput, and energy efficiency.
This review investigates two complementary approaches to improving such systems. First, operating-system and resource-management optimization may improve the efficiency of CPU-based edge workloads. Second, lightweight illumination preprocessing may improve detection robustness under difficult lighting conditions, although it may introduce additional computational overhead.
The review therefore examines existing public safety surveillance frameworks, edge AI optimisation, and illumination-normalisation techniques to identify whether combining system-level optimisation with visual preprocessing can improve overall performance while balancing accuracy, latency, and resource usage.
Literature Review
Public-Safety Surveillance on Resource-Constrained Edge Devices
Automated video surveillance has developed from conventional anomaly-detection models toward increasingly practical edge-based systems. A foundational contribution by Sultani et al. (2018) introduced the UCF-Crime dataset, containing 1,900 long, untrimmed surveillance videos and approximately 128 hours of footage covering 13 real-world anomaly categories, including fighting, robbery, burglary, road accidents, and shooting. The study demonstrated the feasibility of learning anomalous events from weak video-level labels and established a widely used benchmark for realistic surveillance research. However, the primary focus was detection accuracy rather than deployment on computationally constrained devices.
The transition from algorithmic performance toward deployable surveillance has increased interest in edge computing. Patrikar and Parate (2022), in their review of anomaly detection for video surveillance, identified latency as an important concern for time-sensitive surveillance applications and highlighted edge computing as a means of reducing dependence on remote cloud processing. Their review also showed that the intersection between video anomaly detection and edge computing remained comparatively less explored than conventional anomaly-detection research. This distinction is important because a model that performs well on a benchmark does not necessarily satisfy the computational, energy, and response-time constraints of continuous real-world surveillance.
Early work by Baba et al. (2019) demonstrated that violence detection could be performed directly on low-resource hardware. Their smart-city violence-detection system extracted motion-vector information directly from compressed MPEG video and used a cascaded deep-learning architecture, enabling real-time processing on a Raspberry Pi. Rather than improving accuracy through increasingly complex neural architectures, this work reduced the amount of computation required before inference. It therefore illustrates an important principle for embedded public-safety systems: optimisation of the surrounding processing pipeline can be as important as optimisation of the model itself.
More recent approaches have continued this movement toward lightweight edge inference. Khan et al. (2024) proposed VD-Net, an edge-oriented violence-detection framework using lightweight temporal convolutional blocks and bottleneck layers. The framework was evaluated using both surveillance and non-surveillance datasets and reported improvements of approximately 1-4% over the compared state-of-the-art methods. Importantly, VD-Net explicitly treats limited computing capacity as a design constraint rather than considering deployment only after model development. Nevertheless, its main contribution remains model architecture and classification performance rather than optimisation of the operating system or runtime environment on which the model executes.
Recent work has gone further by evaluating complete surveillance pipelines on contemporary single-board computers. Huang et al. (2026) deployed a skeleton-based video anomaly-detection pipeline on a Raspberry Pi 5, incorporating YOLOv8n-Pose, BoT-SORT tracking, and a lightweight anomaly-scoring model. Their implementation demonstrates that practical edge surveillance is not composed of inference alone: detection, tracking, feature processing, and anomaly scoring compete for the same constrained resources. The authors therefore emphasise the need to balance detection accuracy with computational efficiency when deploying full video-analysis pipelines at the edge.
Salman et al. (2026) provide an even closer example of the public-safety workload considered in this review. Their embedded CNN-GRU violence-detection framework was designed for resource-constrained hardware and evaluated across multiple violence and anomaly datasets, including Hockey Fight, RLVS, Violent Flows, ShanghaiTech, and UCF-Crime. The reported implementation achieved an average accuracy of 96.9%, with approximately 26 FPS and 38.4 ms per frame under live-video conditions. Unlike work concerned only with predictive accuracy, their contribution explicitly focuses on the system-level deployment pipeline, including preprocessing, model optimisation, inference, visualisation, and alert generation.
However, the same study also exposes an important limitation of current embedded surveillance systems. Salman et al. report errors under challenging environmental conditions including poor illumination, occlusion, motion blur, and camera-angle variation, and identify adaptive preprocessing and illumination normalisation as possible directions for improvement. Thus, even where real-time inference on embedded hardware becomes achievable, environmental robustness remains unresolved.
Taken together, these studies show a progression from surveillance anomaly recognition toward complete on-device public-safety pipelines. The recent state of the art demonstrates that violence and anomaly detection can operate on Raspberry Pi-class hardware, but deployment introduces two interacting constraints. First, visual degradation such as poor illumination can reduce the reliability of the underlying model. Second, adding additional processing to address these conditions increases the computational burden on hardware that is already resource constrained. This motivates investigation of lightweight preprocessing techniques that improve robustness without making the system impractical for real-time use.
Illumination-Aware Preprocessing for Robust Edge Vision
Low-light conditions are a persistent challenge for computer-vision systems because reduced contrast, noise, and loss of visual detail can degrade downstream detection. One possible solution is to enhance the input before it reaches the detection model. However, for edge systems, enhancement must provide sufficient downstream benefit to justify its additional computational cost.
Li et al. (2021) proposed Zero-Reference Deep Curve Estimation (Zero-DCE), which formulates low-light enhancement as image-specific curve estimation rather than conventional image reconstruction. A significant characteristic of the approach is that training does not require paired low-light and reference images. The authors subsequently introduced Zero-DCE++, a substantially lighter implementation containing approximately 10,000 parameters. Zero-DCE++ was designed specifically to reduce computational requirements while retaining the enhancement capability of Zero-DCE, demonstrating that low-light preprocessing does not necessarily require a large image-to-image neural network.
Liu et al. (2021) investigated the same problem from a Retinex-based perspective with RUAS, which combines model-based unrolling with architecture search to produce a lightweight enhancement network. The work explicitly addresses a weakness of many deep low-light enhancement methods: although they can produce strong visual results, their complex architectures impose substantial computational costs. RUAS therefore targets a better balance between enhancement quality and computation, making it particularly relevant to resource-constrained vision systems.
The relevance of these techniques to surveillance was examined more directly by Abu Awwad et al. (2024). Rather than treating low-light enhancement as an isolated image-processing task, they integrated enhancement into an anomaly-detection pipeline operating across constrained edge devices. Their architecture first classified an image according to its illumination characteristics. Images with sufficient lighting bypassed enhancement, while darker inputs were processed using techniques including Zero-DCE++ and RUAS before object detection. This is conceptually important because it treats preprocessing as conditional, rather than assuming every input should incur the same additional computation.
Their evaluation further demonstrates why this distinction matters. Among the investigated enhancement models, Zero-DCE++ required approximately 0.0013 s per image in the enhancement benchmark while providing strong downstream person-detection performance, whereas RUAS required more computation but produced competitive detection results. In the complete distributed pipeline, processing one image required approximately 0.431 s using Zero-DCE++ and 0.46 s using RUAS. The study therefore demonstrates both sides of the problem: preprocessing can improve visibility and downstream detection, but it becomes another component in the end-to-end latency and resource budget.
Evidence from related safety-critical vision domains suggests that enhancement is also not universally beneficial. Christanto and Miaou (2026) investigated lightweight monocular distance estimation under low-light driving conditions and added CLAHE as an optional luminance enhancement stage. On their Dark-KITTI evaluation, CLAHE reduced RMSE from 10.91 m to 10.55 m. However, under the well-lit KITTI condition, the corresponding error increased from 10.31 m to 10.52 m. Although autonomous driving differs from surveillance-based violence detection, the result demonstrates an important cross-domain principle: illumination preprocessing can provide asymmetric benefits, improving difficult low-light inputs while offering little or even negative benefit under already favourable conditions.
This body of research suggests that illumination enhancement should be regarded as an adaptive systems decision rather than a universally applied preprocessing step. Zero-DCE++ and RUAS demonstrate that lightweight enhancement is feasible, while Abu Awwad et al. show that the decision to enhance can itself be conditioned on the input. However, existing studies primarily optimize enhancement and downstream vision accuracy while treating the underlying operating system and resource allocation as fixed. Additional preprocessing inevitably introduces work-CPU cycles, memory accesses, and potentially increased energy consumption-into an already constrained edge pipeline.
Consequently, illumination enhancement creates a multi-objective optimization problem. The objective is not simply to maximise detection accuracy after enhancement, but to determine whether the improvement in robustness outweighs the additional latency, energy consumption, and resource contention it introduces. Addressing the visual problem alone therefore leaves open a systems question: whether the edge platform itself can be configured more effectively to accommodate the changing workload.
Operating-System and Resource Optimisation for Edge AI
A separate body of research shows that application performance can be improved without changing the underlying neural model. Instead, system-level techniques optimize how CPU time, cores, frequencies, and other computational resources are allocated to a workload.
Han and Lee (2020) proposed Learning Energy Aware Scheduler, an extension of Linux Energy Aware Scheduling for ARM big.LITTLE smartphones. Their approach uses policy-gradient reinforcement learning to adapt scheduler-related parameters according to the characteristics of the running workload. Compared with the baseline Energy Aware Scheduler, the reported experiments reduced power consumption by approximately 2.3-5.7%, improved process-scheduling benchmark performance by as much as 25.5%, and reduced application launch time under high CPU load. The results demonstrate that scheduler configuration is not merely an implementation detail: workload-aware adjustment can influence both performance and energy efficiency. However, the work focuses on general Android applications rather than real-time computer-vision workloads.
Lee et al. (2022) generalised this concept through STUN, an automatic framework that searches Linux scheduler policies and parameters for a target workload. STUN modifies five scheduling policies and ten scheduler parameters, allowing optimization to occur below the application layer without modifying the workload itself. Of particular relevance to computer vision, applying STUN to a face-detection workload reduced execution time by 18.3%, from 58.998 s to 48.198 s, while increasing throughput by 22.4%, from 16.95 to 20.748 FPS. This provides direct evidence that OS-level scheduler configuration can materially affect computer-vision throughput even when the application and model remain unchanged.
However, STUN primarily targets relatively static workloads and its computer-vision evaluation does not reproduce the operating conditions of a constrained public-safety edge device. A continuous surveillance system may change computational state as frames are decoded, illumination preprocessing is activated or bypassed, inference is performed, and events are tracked or logged. Therefore, the optimal system configuration may itself depend on the current state of the vision pipeline.
Research on dynamic voltage and frequency scaling provides further evidence that system configuration has a measurable effect on edge inference. Ng et al. (2023) analysed layer-wise CNN execution on a Raspberry Pi 4 and NVIDIA Jetson Nano under different DVFS configurations. Their experiments found a strong relationship between clock frequency, execution time, and power consumption, demonstrating that inference efficiency on single-board computers depends not only on the network architecture but also on the configuration of the hardware resources executing it.
More recent work has attempted to optimize these parameters dynamically. Zhang et al. (2024) proposed DVFO, which uses deep reinforcement learning to jointly optimize CPU, GPU, and memory frequencies together with edge-cloud inference offloading. Across multiple DNNs and heterogeneous edge devices, the authors report an average energy reduction of approximately 33% and end-to-end latency reductions of up to 28.6-59.1%, while maintaining average model accuracy within approximately 1% of the baseline. Although DVFO considers a different deployment architecture from standalone public-safety surveillance, it reinforces the broader finding that runtime resource configuration can substantially change the latency-energy operating point of edge AI workloads.
The systems literature therefore provides a complementary perspective to illumination-enhancement research. Vision studies generally attempt to improve what information is supplied to the model, whereas scheduler and DVFS studies attempt to improve how computation is executed. Yet these two optimization layers have largely been evaluated independently in the literature considered here.
Synthesis and Research Gap
The three bodies of literature reveal a common problem from different perspectives. First, recent public-safety research demonstrates that violence and anomaly detection can be deployed on resource-constrained edge devices, including Raspberry Pi-class hardware. However, these systems remain vulnerable to real-world visual degradation, particularly poor illumination. Second, lightweight enhancement techniques such as Zero-DCE++, RUAS, and CLAHE can improve the quality of low-light inputs and, in some cases, improve downstream vision performance. However, enhancement is not universally beneficial and adds computational work to the pipeline. Third, operating-system scheduling and resource-management research demonstrates that workload-aware configuration can improve throughput, latency, and energy efficiency without modifying the underlying AI model.
A key gap emerges from the separation of these research directions. The vision literature generally optimises the input while treating the execution environment as fixed, whereas the systems literature optimises the execution environment while treating the application workload and environmental conditions as largely fixed. Within the literature reviewed here, there is limited investigation of these layers jointly for a resource-constrained public-safety surveillance workload.
This separation becomes important because illumination-aware preprocessing changes the computational characteristics of the pipeline. A well-lit frame may proceed directly to inference, while a poorly illuminated frame may require additional classification and enhancement before inference. Consequently, workload intensity can vary according to environmental conditions. A system configuration that is suitable when enhancement is inactive may therefore not provide the best latency, energy, or CPU utilisation when enhancement becomes active.
This motivates a hybrid optimisation approach in which lightweight illumination processing is used to improve robustness under difficult visual conditions, while system-level optimisation is used to manage the additional computational workload introduced by that processing. Importantly, the research objective should not assume that combining both techniques will necessarily improve every metric. The combination may increase accuracy while worsening latency or energy consumption; alternatively, aggressive resource optimisation may recover latency at the cost of additional power. The research problem is therefore to characterise and optimise the trade-off, rather than simply demonstrate that two techniques can be combined.
A suitable evaluation should consequently extend beyond classification accuracy and average FPS. Detection accuracy or F1-score can quantify robustness, while end-to-end latency, throughput, CPU utilisation, power or energy consumption, and percentile latency can characterise system behaviour. Comparing at least four configurations-baseline, illumination optimisation only, system optimisation only, and the combined approach-would make it possible to determine not only whether each intervention is effective, but also whether their interaction produces a better overall operating point for resource-constrained public-safety inference.
Overall, the reviewed research indicates that improving real-world edge surveillance is no longer solely a model-design problem. The current challenge lies in coordinating vision robustness and systems efficiency under changing environmental and computational conditions. Investigating this interaction provides a logical extension of existing work on embedded public-safety inference, low-light enhancement, and workload-aware operating-system optimisation.
